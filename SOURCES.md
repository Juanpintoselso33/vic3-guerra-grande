# Sources

Every piece of text in `localization/` rests on the record below, not on invention.

## The ribbons, 1836

- August 1836: Oribe decrees that the whole population, military and civilian, wear a white
  badge on hat or clothing with the motto **"Defensores de las Leyes"** — the same white the
  patriots of 1811 had worn. Rivera's men took the sky blue of the national cockade, which
  faded white in sun and rain, and replaced it with red cut from the lining of their ponchos.
  - *Origen de las divisas: la batalla de Carpintería*, Contenidos Educativos Digitales, ANEP /
    Plan Ceibal (Uruguay): http://contenidoseducativosdigitales.edu.uy/files/012-origen-de-las-divisas-la-batalla-de-carpinteria.pdf
  - Wikipedia ES, *Batalla de Carpintería*: https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batalla_de_Carpintería
- 19 September 1836, arroyo Carpintería (Durazno): some 2,200–3,000 men under Oribe and Lavalleja
  against some 1,400 under Rivera and the exiled Argentine unitarian Juan Lavalle. A blanco victory.

## Two governments

- Oribe resigns on 24 October 1838 and goes on claiming the presidency from Buenos Aires; the siege
  of Montevideo begins on **16 February 1843** and lasts until October 1851.
- The Cerrito Government sat on the hill beside the **Villa de la Restauración** (today La Unión,
  Montevideo), reconvened the Chambers Rivera had dissolved in 1838 and had them renew Oribe's
  mandate. The Defensa Government had **Joaquín Suárez** as interim president for eight years.
  - Wikipedia ES, *Guerra Grande*: https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guerra_Grande
  - Intendencia de Montevideo, Municipio E, *La Unión*: https://municipioe.montevideo.gub.uy/node/160
- Decree of the Defensa on the day the siege begins: **"El ejército de Rosas está delante de esta
  capital. El Gobierno cuenta con el patriotismo de sus habitantes: reposa en él y en la victoria."**
  - *Sitio de Montevideo (1843-1851)*, revisionistas.com.ar: https://www.revisionistas.com.ar?p=8169

## The blockade

- The Anglo-French blockade of the Río de la Plata, also known as the **Guerra del Paraná**,
  2 August 1845 – 31 August 1850: every port of the Argentine Confederation closed to trade
  except Montevideo. Battle of Vuelta de Obligado, November 1845. It ended with the
  **Arana-Southern** (1849/1850) and **Arana-Lepredour** treaties, in which Britain and France
  acknowledged Argentine sovereignty over the Paraná and the Uruguay.
  - Wikipedia ES, *Bloqueo anglo-francés del Río de la Plata*: https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloqueo_anglo-francés_del_Río_de_la_Plata
  - Wikipedia EN, *Anglo-French blockade of the Río de la Plata*: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-French_blockade_of_the_Río_de_la_Plata

## Garibaldi and the red shirts

- The Italian Legion is raised in Montevideo in 1843. The most often repeated explanation of the red
  shirt is economic: a cheap lot of shirts intended as working clothes for the saladeros across the
  river, left unsold. Another version credits the painter Gaetano Gallino.
  - Wikipedia ES, *Camisas rojas*: https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camisas_rojas

## The New Troy

- Alexandre Dumas, **_Montevideo, ou une nouvelle Troie_** (Paris, Imprimerie Centrale de
  Napoléon Chaix et Cie, 1850, 174 pp.), serialised in *Le Mois*. Dumas never set foot in
  Uruguay: he wrote from the reports of **Melchor Pacheco y Obes**, sent to Paris by the
  Defensa to win French support, which is why the book is openly partisan against Oribe,
  Rosas and Artigas. The in-game event says exactly that.
  - Wikipedia ES, *Montevideo o la Nueva Troya*: https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montevideo_o_la_Nueva_Troya
  - Área de Museos, MEC (Uruguay), *Alexandre Dumas, Giuseppe Garibaldi y Montevideo*:
    https://museos.gub.uy/index.php/component/k2/item/1354-alejandro-dumas-y-jose-garibaldi

## The four portraits

Flavour events between Carpintería (1836) and Oribe's fall (1838).

- **Oribe**: born in Montevideo in 1792; second in command of the Thirty-Three Orientals; member of the
  Caballeros Orientales lodge; a legalist who backed President Rivera against Lavalleja's risings of
  1832 and 1834; refused Rosas's suggestion that Uruguay rejoin the Argentine Confederation.
  - Wikipedia ES, *Manuel Oribe*: https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuel_Oribe
  - *Manuel Oribe, el militar, el estadista, el masón*, gadu.org:
    https://www.gadu.org/antologia/manuel-oribe-el-militar-el-estadista-el-mason/
- **Lavalleja**: the landing at La Agraciada, 19 April 1825; made Captain General by the Congress of
  Florida; rose against Rivera in 1832 and 1834 and was beaten both times; fought on the government side
  at Carpintería with nearly all of the Thirty-Three.
- **Rivera**: son of a landowner, barely schooled, Artigas's most effective chief; Tres Árboles
  (1820) and service under Lecor; declined the title of Barón de Tacuarembó; the embrace of
  Monzón; took the Misiones Orientales; first president, governing from Durazno. The British consul
  **Thomas Hood, 1829**: *"Es un hombre sin ninguna clase de talento, pero es popular entre la
  mayoría del pueblo"*.
  - Wikipedia ES, *Fructuoso Rivera*: https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fructuoso_Rivera
  - *Uruguay nació saqueado y dividido en dos bandos*, Montevideo Portal:
    https://www.montevideo.com.uy/El-nacimiento-del-Uruguay-moderno/Uruguay-nacio-saqueado-y-dividido-en-dos-bandos-Lavalleja-y-Rivera-uc891781
- **Artigas**: crossed the Paraná on 5 September 1820 at Itapuá; months at La Merced, then
  twenty-five years confined at San Isidro de Curuguaty; Francia never received him and never
  answered his letters, but sent him a gold ounce a month, which he shared with the poor (an oral
  tradition recorded by Father Fidel Maíz for Zorrilla de San Martín); he farmed, and prayed the rosary
  with neighbours who answered in Guaraní; in 1841 the consuls López and Roque Alonso told him he
  was free to return, and he asked to stay; died at Ibiray on 23 September 1850, aged 86.
  - Tomás Sansón Corbo, *Morir en Paraguay. Exilio y destino de José Artigas*, ABC Color:
    https://www.abc.com.py/edicion-impresa/suplementos/cultural/2020/09/13/morir-en-paraguay-exilio-y-destino-de-jose-artigas/
  - Dardo Ramírez Braschi, *200 años del ingreso de José Gervasio Artigas al Paraguay* (UCA):
    https://repositorio.uca.edu.ar/bitstream/123456789/10994/1/200-anos-ingreso-artigas-uruguay.pdf
  - *El exilio en Paraguay*, Contenidos Educativos Digitales (ANEP/Ceibal):
    http://contenidoseducativosdigitales.edu.uy/files/047-el-exilio-en-paraguay.pdf

Neither Rosas nor Brazil declared war on the Republic before 1851. Rosas recognised Oribe as
Uruguay's lawful president and lent him the Ejército Unido de Vanguardia; Brazil held to neutrality and
paid the Defensa in secret through Mauá's loans from 1849, signing the alliance only on 29 May 1851.
That is why the submod gives battalions and subsidies instead of diplomatic plays.

## The northeastern border before 1851

The Convención Preliminar de Paz of 1828 fixed **no** boundary. Until the limits treaty of
12 October 1851 the Republic still claimed, on the titles of San Ildefonso (1777) and the act of
incorporation of 1821, the waters of the Laguna Merín and the Río Yaguarón and the old *Campos
Neutrales* between the Chuy and San Miguel passes — held in fact by Brazilian settlers since 1801.
The 1851 treaty gave Brazil exclusive navigation of the lagoon and left Uruguay a **dry shore** for
58 years, until the Laguna Merín treaty of 30 October 1909. The Misiones Orientales had already been
lost in practice in 1828. The submod models this as a Uruguayan claim on Rio Grande do Sul that the
treaties of 1851 extinguish.

## The Uruguayan map in 1836

Every state and hub name in the submod is checked against two independent things: the real
coordinate of the town, and its documented year of foundation. The georeference was fitted by
matching the silhouette of Uruguay from the geoBoundaries ADM1 dataset against the game's own
province map, reaching an intersection-over-union of 0.95; town coordinates come from the GeoNames
national gazetteer (`UY.txt`, retrieved 16 September 2026, CC BY 4.0).

**Base map.** Outside the five oriental states, the South American map, history and locators are
«la Argentina»'s (Workshop 2982907360), merged from the old compatch on 24/9/2026; what each file
takes from it is listed in [`CREDITS.md`](CREDITS.md).

### The nine departments of 1836

The Constitution of 1830 recognised nine departments **without fixing a single boundary**;
the only department with legally defined limits in 1836 was Montevideo, whose line was restored by
Ley 84 of 1835 and is still the modern one. The nine: Montevideo · Canelones · **San José**
(including today's Florida and Flores) · Colonia · Soriano (capital **Villa Soriano**, not Mercedes,
until 1857) · **Maldonado** (including Rocha and southern Lavalleja) · **Cerro Largo** (including
Treinta y Tres and northern Lavalleja) · Durazno · **Paysandú** (everything north of the Río Negro:
today's Artigas, Rivera, Tacuarembó, Salto, Paysandú and Río Negro, c. 74,000 km²). Salto,
Tacuarembó and Minas were split off on 16 June 1837 by Ley 158 — one year into the game.

Sources: IDE Uruguay (AGESIC), *Límites departamentales del Uruguay — Informe diagnóstico*,
October 2024, pp. 3–5 and 25–26; Intendencia de Montevideo, *Montevideo: de marca fronteriza a capital
de un Estado*, fascicle 3, pp. 66–68; ANEP, *A 193 años de la Jura de la primera Constitución*, 2023.

### Population

Some 74,000 inhabitants is a contemporary estimate of the late 1820s (Andrés Lamas, *Cálculo
estadístico de junio de 1829*), not a census. The often-quoted "131,000 in 1843" is wrong: 131,969
is the total of the **first national census, that of 1852**. The 31,189 of 1843 is Montevideo inside
the siege lines alone, of whom roughly two thirds were foreign-born.

Sources: Raquel Pollero, *Historia demográfica de Montevideo y su campaña, 1757-1860*,
UdelaR, pp. 149, 179–184 and 268–269; INE, *Cuadros censales comparativos, 1852-2011*.

## Economic geography

What the states produce was corrected against the historical record, not guessed.

| Change | Why |
|---|---|
| **Sealing concentrated in Maldonado** (whaling 4 → 8; lowered in the other three) | Sea lions were taken on the **Isla de Lobos and at Cabo Polonio**, and sealskin was the first American product exported to Europe. **Francisco Aguilar obtained the state concession in 1820, for twenty years**: oil and skins, with England as the main market |
| **Iron moves from a cap to an undiscovered deposit** (Cerro Largo 4, Paysandú 7, and San José 4 added) | Uruguay's iron is that of **Valentines** — where Florida, Durazno and Treinta y Tres meet — and of **Zapucay, Rivera**. The rocks were identified only in **1915** (Marstrander) and the deposits defined in the 1950s and 1960s. **It was never worked.** As a cap available in 1836 it is an anachronism; as an undiscovered deposit it is exact |
| **Vineyards added to Paysandú** | Uruguayan viticulture begins in **Salto with Pascual Harriague (1874)**, and the northern littoral remains the main wine region |
| **Oil removed from all three states** | **No oil has ever been found** in Uruguay: hydrocarbon exploration «no se ha materializado en descubrimientos». What did turn up, in the 1950s, was the thermal water of the Guaraní Aquifer |
| **Gold stays in Paysandú** (gold_mine 4) | **Cuñapirú and Minas de Corrales**, in today's Rivera (then Tacuarembó): prospectors from the 1840s and 1850s, industrial working with French and British capital from 1867, and the **first hydroelectric dam in Uruguay, and possibly in South America**. Some 100,000 ounces in that first phase |
| **Cattle unchanged** | The saladeros stood where the mod already places them: Manuel Melián's, the first, on the **río San Salvador (1780)**, Francisco de Medina's on the **arroyo Colla (1787)**, **Francisco A. Maciel's on the Miguelete**, and later Lafone's at La Teja. Always beside a port and a watercourse |

One figure worth knowing for the war itself: Uruguay had some **6.5 million head of cattle
when the Guerra Grande began in 1839, and about two million when it ended in 1851**. The war drove
stock-raising back to its most primitive forms — slaughter for subsistence, mass killing of cattle
and horses to pay for arms and debts, contraband hides, and the great overland drives into Rio Grande
do Sul.

Sources: F. Cernuschi, F. González, M. Morales Demarco, J. Marmisolle, B. Conti and J. Bossi,
«Breve historia de la exploración y explotación de recursos minerales y energéticos en Uruguay»,
Sociedad Uruguaya de Geología, 2024 (doi 10.69702/21.1); E. Palermo, «Paisaje cultural minero: la
región aurífera de Minas de Corrales», *Estudios Históricos* n.º 22; A. Turell Lorenzo, *Aproximación
histórica y cultural a la Isla de Lobos*, UdelaR, 2023; *Revista Histórica Rochense* n.º 9, «Crónicas
del Cabo Polonio: la lobería, el puerto y la aduana»; «Los saladeros, una industria rústica y
hedionda que contribuyó a crear un nuevo Estado», series *El nacimiento del Uruguay moderno*, 2024.
